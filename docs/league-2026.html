Everything Counts

A starter used to be able to play sixty snaps, catch nothing, and be worth exactly as much as a guy on a bye. Not any more. This year the scoresheet stopped ignoring things.

The big one: four flex slots

One slot at each offensive position, then four flex slots that take a quarterback, running back or receiver. Tight end keeps a slot of its own.

This only works if no position is obviously the best one, so the scoring below levels them out. The average quarterback, back and receiver are now worth within a point of each other per start.

Which means: draft the best player, not the best player at a position you're short of. Positional scarcity mostly stops driving your board.

Scoring: three changes

Yahoo asks how many yards make a point, so fewer yards is more points. Nothing anywhere in these rules was reduced.

Why receivers got the biggest raise

Not because receivers are now the best position — quarterbacks still score most per start. Quarterback scoring simply didn't need to move. Passing yards and passing touchdowns are untouched, so the other two had to climb to meet them.

Kickers, by the yard

The five distance brackets are gone. A made field goal is now worth one point per 10 yards, full stop.

Kickers score the same on average — the old brackets already paid almost exactly a tenth of a point per yard. What changes is that a 49‑yarder finally beats a 40‑yarder, where the brackets paid both four points, and a 39‑yarder no longer pays the same as a chip shot.

The chip shot

Five points for missing from inside twenty yards. It has never happened. There have been 305 attempts that short since 1999 and a kicker has missed exactly none of them — the only two that failed were blocked, in 2001 and 2002.

So this category is not really a penalty. It is a bounty on the most embarrassing thing a kicker can do, and if anyone in this league collects it we will talk about it for years.

The participation trophy manual

Play a snap, score something

Any starter who takes at least one offensive snap and finishes on exactly zero gets credited with 10 points instead. Same for every position.

Special teams doesn't count — you have to have been out there on offense. And exactly zero means exactly zero: fumble your way to minus two and you get nothing. No trophy for being actively bad.

Yahoo can't do this one, so I'll apply it by hand. Expect it about 18 times a season across the league — once or twice a week, almost always a receiver or tight end.

What this actually does

Every one of these was tested against six real NFL seasons before it made the list, replaying thousands of seasons under the old rules and the new ones to see what changed.

The whole package flips about one game in sixteen. It narrows the gap between the best and worst records slightly, and nudges the underdog win rate up. Most importantly, the relationship between scoring points and winning games is unchanged — the league gets more varied without getting more random. Nothing here is a lottery.

Then I checked it against us. Every lineup anyone in this league actually set over the last three seasons — 210 real matchups — rescored under the new rules. One game in thirteen would have gone the other way, which is the simulation's answer within the margin of error. That test can only measure the scoring changes and not the new flex slots, because nobody drafted for those.

And no, you can't beat it by filling all four flex slots with quarterbacks. I checked. It costs you about half a win, because you run out of good ones.
